Checkpoint question 3:

| Item | English |
|---|---|
| Question | Jimin's AHT is longer than the team average. What is the right way to prepare for coaching? |
| Option a (wrong) | Set the goal of cutting her AHT to the team average. |
| a feedback | Aiming only at time can shake what Jimin already does well: resolving contacts the first time. |
| Option b (right) | Look at her AHT, her first-time resolution and a few recordings together. |
| Option c (wrong) | Prepare a ranking of AHT by team member and show it to her. |
| c feedback | A ranking reads as a comparison and tends to make her defensive, and it says nothing about why her time runs long. |
| Explanation | Looking at one metric alone easily turns coaching into trading one metric for another. Several metrics and real contacts together lead to the real cause. |

## ③ New SOP: From colleague to team leader

| Item | English |
|---|---|
| Title | From Colleague to Team Leader |
| Short name | First 90 days |
| Subtitle | Your first 90 days leading the colleagues you used to work beside. |
| Who follows it | New team leader, assistant manager |
| Case person | Haeun — the new team leader's closest colleague |
| Nodes | Listen · Agree on standards · Act and check · 90-day review |

Steps:

1. This procedure exists to move you, once you lead the colleagues you used to work beside, into the role of leading the team without losing their trust. The new team leader and the assistant manager responsible for them follow it. It runs in three steps.
2. From today you lead the colleagues you used to take contacts beside on the same team. Until yesterday you had lunch together, so everyone is watching how you will change.
3. The first step is listening (Example: first 30 days). Do not change things right away; meet each team member one on one and ask what is going well now and what is hard.
4. Watch the rules and instructions you inherited first, and understand why they are done that way. Change them without knowing why, and the team feels the new leader does not know the floor.
5. With your assistant manager, agree first on what they expect of a team leader and how far you can decide on your own.
6. The second step is agreeing on standards (Example: days 31 to 60). Talk with the team to set the standards it will keep together, such as attendance, quality and how to ask for help, and say clearly that they apply to everyone the same way.
7. The closer the colleague, the more care it takes. Shield Haeun, your closest colleague, or treat her harder on purpose, and the whole team feels things are not fair.
8. The third step is acting and checking (Example: days 61 to 90). Run one-on-one coaching and team huddles on a set rhythm, and change the procedures you want to change one at a time, after talking them over with the team.
9. When you make a mistake, own it first. When a new team leader hides mistakes, the team starts hiding theirs too.
10. At 90 days, look back together with your assistant manager. Review what the team members say and the team's metrics side by side, and decide what to do in the next 90 days.

Checkpoint:

| Item | English |
|---|---|
| Question 1 | In your first week as team leader, one inherited procedure looks inefficient. What should you do first? |
| Option a (wrong) | Switch to a new way today. |
| a feedback | Changing it without knowing why makes the team feel it was decided without them, and the problem the procedure was preventing can come back. |
| Option b (right) | Talk with the team and first understand why it has been done that way. |
| Option c (wrong) | Know the problem but say nothing until 90 days have passed. |
| c feedback | The listening period is for asking and understanding. Bury a problem you have found, and the team decides that speaking up is pointless. |
| Explanation | In the first step you listen and understand rather than change. A change is accepted when you know the reason and have talked it over with the team. |
| Question 2 | Haeun, your closest colleague, keeps arriving late. What is the best way to handle it? |
| Option a (wrong) | You are close, so let it go this time. |
| a feedback | When the standard is looser for one person, the rest of the team feels the standard depends on who you are. |
| Option b (right) | Talk with her calmly in a one-on-one, by the same standard as everyone else. |
| Option c (wrong) | Call her out sternly in front of the team to make an example of her. |
| c feedback | Calling her out in public hurts her, and the team reads it as you cutting off the friendship on purpose. |
| Explanation | Apply the agreed standards the same way to everyone, but in a way that shows respect. |
